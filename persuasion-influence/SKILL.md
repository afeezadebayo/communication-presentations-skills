# Persuasion and Influence

Most important work needs other people to agree: a budget approved, a priority changed, a process adopted, a partner convinced. Many capable people lose these moments not because their idea is weak but because they argue from their own view, approach the wrong person at the wrong time, ignore what the other side cares about or rely on one meeting to change minds. Research and practice point to a few reliable foundations. Aristotle described credibility (ethos), emotion (pathos) and logic (logos). Robert Cialdini's research on influence identified principles such as reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity. [Reference, Cialdini, Influence] Roger Fisher and William Ury's principled negotiation separates interests from positions and stresses knowing your best alternative. [Reference, Getting to Yes]

This skill helps people influence decisions ethically, especially without formal authority: mapping stakeholders, understanding their interests and objections, framing proposals around shared goals, building evidence and credibility, choosing the right channel and sequence, handling resistance, negotiating trade-offs and securing commitment that lasts.

## Operating principles
1. **Start from their interests.** People agree to what solves their problems, not yours.
2. **Earn credibility before asking.** Track record, expertise, preparation and fairness make ideas easier to accept.
3. **Logic, emotion and trust together.** Data persuades the head; stories and stakes move people; trust lets both land.
4. **Influence is a process, not a meeting.** Pre-wire key people one to one before group decisions.
5. **Ethical only.** Never mislead, manipulate, invent urgency or hide downsides; influence that relies on tricks destroys trust.
6. **Make yes easy.** Small first steps, clear asks and low-risk pilots.

## Stakeholder map
| Stakeholder | Role in decision | Current view | What they care about | Concerns | Best messenger | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CFO | Approver | Sceptical | Cost, payback, risk | Budget overrun | Finance partner | One-to-one with payback model |
| Head of sales | Influencer | Supportive | Pipeline, rep time | Disruption in Q4 | Sales ops lead | Ask to co-present |
| IT lead | Must agree | Neutral | Security, workload | Integration effort | Me | Share security review |

Use a power and interest grid: manage closely those with high power and high interest, keep satisfied those with high power and low interest, keep informed those with low power and high interest.

## Influence principles in practice (ethical use)
| Principle | Ethical application | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Help others with their goals first; share useful work | Favours given as hidden debts |
| Commitment and consistency | Ask for small, public steps that match stated goals | Trapping people in commitments |
| Social proof | Show what similar teams or companies did and their results | Inventing or exaggerating adoption |
| Authority | Bring expert evidence or credible sponsors | Name-dropping without substance |
| Liking | Build real relationships and common ground | Flattery |
| Scarcity | Explain real deadlines or opportunity costs | False urgency |
| Unity | Frame the shared identity and goal ("our customers", "our team") | Us-versus-them pressure |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it for getting proposals approved, changing priorities, influencing peers and senior leaders, cross-team projects, managing up, negotiating resources and winning support for change.

Don't use it to pressure people into decisions against their interests, to manipulate, or where formal processes (procurement, HR, legal) must decide on merit.

## Inputs
- What you want decided or changed, and by when.
- Who decides, who influences and who must agree.
- What you know about each person's goals, pressures and views.
- Evidence: data, examples, pilots, expert input.
- Your credibility and relationships with each stakeholder.
- Alternatives if the answer is no.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Define the ask precisely
What decision, from whom, by when, and the smallest acceptable version (for example a pilot instead of a full rollout).

### Step 2. Map stakeholders
Roles in the decision, current views, interests, concerns and relationships between them.

### Step 3. Understand interests behind positions
For each key person, ask: what are they measured on, what are they worried about, what would make this a win for them?

### Step 4. Frame around shared goals
Connect the proposal to their priorities: "This cuts onboarding time, which helps the retention target you own."

### Step 5. Build the case
Evidence (data, examples, pilot results), a clear story of the problem and the better future, risks with mitigations and the cost of doing nothing.

### Step 6. Pre-wire one to one
Talk to key people before the group meeting; listen to concerns; adapt the proposal; ask for their support or at least no surprises.

### Step 7. Choose messenger and channel
Sometimes a trusted peer, customer or expert is more persuasive than you. Pick written proposals for analytical audiences and conversations for relationship-driven ones.

### Step 8. Handle objections
Acknowledge, ask questions to understand, address the underlying concern, offer options or a smaller first step.

### Step 9. Ask clearly and secure commitment
State the ask, confirm the decision and next steps in writing, and give people a role in success.

### Step 10. Follow through
Deliver early results, credit supporters publicly and report progress; trust built now makes the next ask easier.

## Framing examples
| Your view | Their interest | Reframed |
|---|---|---|
| "We need a new analytics tool" | CFO: cost control | "Consolidating three tools into one saves £18,000 a year and cuts reporting time" |
| "Engineering should fix onboarding bugs" | CTO: roadmap focus | "Two fixes remove 30% of support tickets, freeing engineers from interruptions" |
| "Marketing needs more budget" | CEO: growth target | "£20,000 more in paid search adds about 60 qualified meetings toward the Q4 target" |

## Handling common objections
| Objection | Response approach |
|---|---|
| "We don't have budget" | Show payback, cost of waiting, or a smaller funded pilot |
| "Now isn't the right time" | Ask what would make it the right time; agree a date or trigger |
| "We tried this before" | Ask what happened; show what is different now |
| "It's too risky" | Name risks, show mitigations, propose a reversible test |
| "Not my priority" | Link to their goals or find who it is a priority for |
| "I need to think about it" | Ask what information would help; agree when to reconnect |

## Negotiating trade-offs
- Know your best alternative if no agreement is reached, and theirs.
- Separate the person from the problem; stay respectful under pressure.
- Trade things that are cheap for you and valuable for them.
- Use objective criteria (benchmarks, data, policies) rather than will.
- Write down agreements and owners.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| No formal authority | Build coalitions; borrow authority from sponsors; show results first |
| Senior leader opposes | Understand their concern privately; adapt; escalate only with respect and data |
| Group decision with competing interests | Pre-wire, find shared goals, offer options, let the decider decide |
| Repeated rejection | Ask for feedback; reconsider the idea or timing; build evidence with a small test |
| Cultural differences | Adjust directness, hierarchy and pace; learn local norms |
| Ethical doubt | Stop; influence that requires hiding information is the wrong path |

## Worked example
A product operations manager wanted leadership to fund a customer onboarding redesign. Her first attempt, a 20-slide deck in the quarterly review, was deferred. Using this skill she mapped stakeholders: the CFO cared about payback, the head of customer success about churn, the CTO about engineering time. She reframed the proposal as a retention lever with a payback estimate, ran a two-week pilot with ten new customers that cut setup time by half, and pre-wired each leader one to one, adjusting the plan to use a contractor so engineering time was protected. The head of customer success co-presented the results. The proposal was approved in the next meeting with a phased budget and a review after one quarter.

## Common mistakes
- Arguing from your own interests.
- Relying on one big meeting.
- Ignoring the quiet blocker with veto power.
- Overstating benefits or hiding risks.
- Not following through after a yes.

## Output format
```
1. The ask and smallest acceptable version
2. Stakeholder map with interests and concerns
3. Framing for each key stakeholder
4. Evidence and case summary
5. Pre-wiring plan (who, when, what)
6. Objections and responses
7. Commitment and follow-through plan
```

## Self-check before you answer
- Is the proposal framed around the decision makers' interests?
- Are key people pre-wired before the group decision?
- Are evidence and risks presented openly?
- Are all tactics ethical, with no false urgency or hidden downsides?
