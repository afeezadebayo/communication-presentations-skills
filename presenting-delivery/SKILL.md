# Presenting and Delivery

A strong storyline and clear slides can still fall flat in delivery. Presenters read their slides, rush through the important parts, run over time, lose the room on a video call or freeze when a senior person asks a hard question. Most of this is fixed by preparation rather than talent: rehearsing out loud, knowing the opening and closing word for word, planning for questions and adapting to the room or the call.

This skill prepares a person to deliver a presentation well: speaker notes, timed rehearsals, openings and closings, voice, pace and pauses, body language and eye contact, handling nerves, running video calls, managing interaction, answering questions (including hostile or off-topic ones), recovering from problems and following up afterwards.

## Operating principles
1. **Rehearse out loud.** Thinking it through is not rehearsal; speaking it is.
2. **Know the first and last minute by heart.** Strong starts and ends carry the middle.
3. **Talk to people, not slides.** Glance at the slide, then speak to the audience.
4. **Finish early.** Plan to end with spare minutes for discussion.
5. **Prepare for questions.** Most questions can be predicted; answer them in advance.

## Inputs
- The deck and storyline.
- Time slot, format (room, video call, hybrid, recording) and audience.
- The presenter's experience and concerns.
- Likely questions and any sensitive topics.

## When to use, and when not to
Use it before leadership updates, pitches, conference talks, training, interviews with presentations, all-hands and client meetings.

Don't use it to build the story (`presentation-storyline`) or the slides (`slide-design`).

## Workflow

### Step 1. Write speaker notes
For each slide: the key message in one line, the evidence to point to, a transition sentence to the next slide and any number to stress. Keep notes as prompts, not a script, except for the opening and closing.

### Step 2. Script the opening and closing
Write the first 30 to 60 seconds and the final call to action word for word and memorise them.

### Step 3. Rehearse with a timer
At least three full out-loud rehearsals for important talks: one to find problems, one to fix timing, one at real pace. Record one on video and watch it back.

### Step 4. Cut to fit
Aim to finish in about 80% of the slot. Mark slides to skip if time runs short.

### Step 5. Prepare questions
List the ten most likely questions, including the hardest, with short answers and backup slides.

### Step 6. Check the setup
In a room: screen, clicker, laptop power, sound, where to stand. On a call: camera at eye level, lighting from the front, microphone, a clean background, presenter view working, notifications off.

### Step 7. Deliver
Open strongly, pause after key points, look at people (or the camera), move through slides at the planned pace and check understanding.

### Step 8. Handle questions
Listen fully, repeat or rephrase the question, answer briefly, check it was answered and return to the message.

### Step 9. Close and follow up
Restate the call to action, thank people, share the deck and notes, and confirm decisions and next steps in writing.

## Speaker notes format
```
Slide 3: Lead quality matches search
Say: conversion to opportunity is 21% for both channels.
Point to: the two equal bars.
Stress: "same quality, half the cost".
Transition: "So if quality holds, why hasn't search got cheaper? Because..."
Likely question: "Is the sample big enough?" -> 1,240 leads over six months.
```

## Voice, pace and body language
| Area | Practice |
|---|---|
| Pace | Slower than conversation for key points; about 130 to 150 words a minute overall |
| Pauses | Two to three seconds after a key number or question |
| Volume | Project to the back row; on calls, speak slightly louder and clearer |
| Filler words | Replace "um" with a pause; record and count in rehearsal |
| Eye contact | Hold each person for a full sentence; on calls, look at the camera when making key points |
| Hands and stance | Open, still feet, gestures that match the words |
| Energy | Slightly more than feels natural, especially online |

## Handling nerves
- Prepare more than you think you need; preparation reduces anxiety more than anything else.
- Arrive or log in early to test equipment and settle.
- Breathe slowly before starting: in for four, out for six.
- Have water nearby; pause to drink when you need a moment.
- Focus on helping the audience, not on being judged.
- Know that small mistakes are rarely noticed; keep going.

## Video call and hybrid presentations
- Share only the slide window, not the whole screen; turn notifications off.
- Keep slides simpler and text larger than in a room.
- Ask a colleague to watch chat and raise hands.
- Pause for questions every few minutes; call on people by name kindly.
- In hybrid meetings, address remote people first in discussions so they are not forgotten.
- Record only with consent and share the recording with the deck.

## Answering questions
| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| Clear question | Answer in one or two sentences, then offer detail |
| You do not know | Say so, say how you will find out and when you will reply |
| Hostile question | Stay calm, acknowledge the concern, answer the substance, avoid defensiveness |
| Off-topic question | Acknowledge it, offer to discuss after, return to the agenda |
| Long statement disguised as a question | Thank them, summarise the point, respond briefly |
| Senior person disagrees | Ask what would change their view; offer data or a follow-up |
| Silence | Ask a specific question to the room or start with one you prepared |

A useful pattern for hard questions: listen, pause, rephrase ("So the concern is..."), answer the core point, check ("Does that address it?").

## Recovering from problems
| Problem | Recovery |
|---|---|
| Technology fails | Carry on with the key points from memory; share the deck afterwards |
| Running over time | Skip marked slides; go to the close and the ask |
| Lost your place | Pause, look at notes, summarise the last point, continue |
| Audience disengaged | Ask a question, tell a short story or move to the decision |
| Wrong number spotted | Acknowledge, correct it and follow up in writing |

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Presenting in a second language | Simpler sentences, slower pace, more rehearsal, key phrases memorised |
| Very senior audience | Fewer slides, start with the answer, expect interruptions and welcome them |
| Large conference | Rehearse on the stage if possible; bigger gestures; no small text |
| Recorded video | Shorter, tighter, more energy, look at the lens |
| Panel discussion | Prepare three key messages and short stories; keep answers under a minute |
| Team presentation | Rehearse handovers between speakers; one owner of time |

## Worked example
A product manager had to present a roadmap change to the leadership team and usually ran over time. The skill turned her 18-slide deck into speaker notes with transitions, scripted the opening and the ask, and ran three timed rehearsals: the first took 26 minutes for a 20-minute slot, so three slides moved to the appendix. A list of ten likely questions surfaced a hard one about a delayed customer commitment, which got a backup slide. On the day she finished in 16 minutes, answered the hard question calmly with the backup slide and left time for the decision, which was approved.

## Common mistakes
- Rehearsing silently or not at all.
- Reading slides aloud.
- Running over time and rushing the ending.
- Being surprised by predictable questions.
- Ending with "Any questions?" instead of the ask.

## Output format
```
1. Speaker notes for each slide
2. Scripted opening and closing
3. Rehearsal plan with timings and slides to cut
4. Likely questions with answers and backup slides
5. Setup checklist (room or call)
6. Follow-up message draft
```

## Self-check before you answer
- Are the opening and close scripted?
- Is the talk timed to finish early, with slides marked to skip?
- Are the ten most likely questions answered?
- Is the setup checked for the room or call?
